2023
Eleven buildings, one terrible secret. From the masked Japanese author whose mysteries are assembled out of architectural floor plans, where the horror is in what the layout implies. Blurb still to be written.

The Final Testament of the Holy Bible

James Frey

398 pages

2011
The Messiah has returned, and he is a brain-damaged construction worker in a condemned Bronx building. Frey never lets Ben Zion Avrohom speak for himself: the book is testimony from the people who knew him, a brother, a landlord, a prostitute, a rabbi, each account contradicting the last.

We Used to Live Here

Marcus Kliewer

312 pages

2024
Eve and her girlfriend Charlie flip houses, and they have just bought a big old place in the woods. Then a man knocks: he grew up here, his family is in the car, could they look around, just for a minute. Eve says yes. They do not leave.

One's Company

Ashley Hutson

272 pages

2022
Bonnie Lincoln survives something unspeakable, wins an enormous lottery jackpot, and spends it building a private replica of the Three's Company sets on remote land, where she intends to live out the sitcom alone, playing every part on a rotating schedule. Grief as world-building.

The Lost City of the Monkey God

Douglas Preston

326 pages

2017
Preston joins an expedition into Honduras's Mosquitia, one of the last unmapped rainforests, hunting a city generations of adventurers died looking for. Lidar finds it from the air; reaching it on foot means snakes and mud and a valley no human has entered in five centuries. Then the team starts coming home with a flesh-eating parasite.

American Psycho

Bret Easton Ellis

399 pages

1991
Patrick Bateman, 26, Pierce & Pierce, reviews his skincare regimen and his business cards in the same flat voice he uses for the murders, and nobody in his circle can reliably tell him apart from the other interchangeable men in Valentino. Whether any of it happens is left open.

Remarkably Bright Creatures

Shelby Van Pelt

360 pages

2022
Tova cleans the aquarium at night because her husband is dead and her son vanished off a boat thirty years ago and the work keeps her upright. Marcellus, a giant Pacific octopus with a low opinion of humans and about a year left to live, has worked out what happened to the boy. The problem is telling her.

We Love You, Bunny

Mona Awad

496 pages

2025
The sequel to Bunny, and this time the Bunnies get the microphone. They have a great deal to say about Samantha, about what really went on in that workshop, and about who was using whom. Cotton-candy menace at nearly five hundred pages.

The Long Walk

Richard Bachman / Stephen King

370 pages

1979
One hundred teenage boys start walking south from the Maine border. Drop below four miles an hour and you get a warning; collect three and the soldiers on the halftrack shoot you. No finish line, only a last boy standing. King wrote it at eighteen and it is still the meanest thing he has published.

I Who Have Never Known Men

Jacqueline Harpman

201 pages

1995
Thirty-nine women in a cage in a bunker, guarded by silent men, and one girl among them too young to remember anything before. She has no reference for the world the others mourn. Then the alarm sounds, the guards run, and the cage is open. A cold, clean book about being the last of a species that never taught you what it was.

The Compound

Aisling Rawle

292 pages

2025
Lily wakes in a desert compound with nineteen other contestants, ten men and ten women, on a reality show nobody explains to her. Completing tasks earns luxuries; the tasks get worse. Outside, something has gone wrong with the world, though the show does not discuss that either.

Banal Nightmare

Halle Butler

315 pages

2024
Margaret Anne moves back to her Midwestern college town after leaving a bad relationship in Chicago, planning to recover. Instead she is thirty-something among people she went to school with, all of them monstrous in the small way real people are, all of them talking. Butler's specialty is the interior monologue that will not let anyone off.

Children of Time

Adrian Tchaikovsky

599 pages

2015
A terraforming project seeds a planet with a virus meant to uplift monkeys. The monkeys die and the spiders get it instead. Millennia pass in jumps as an arachnid civilisation invents language, religion and mathematics, while the last human ark ship limps toward the only habitable world left, which is theirs.

Sky Daddy

Kate Folk

352 pages

2025
Linda is sexually attracted to aeroplanes and believes she is destined to die in a crash, which will be her wedding. She works a dead-end social media job to fund flights, ranks aircraft by how they look at her, and narrates all of it with total sincerity. Somehow funny rather than cruel.

The Clan of the Cave Bear

Jean M. Auel

494 pages

1980
An earthquake orphans a five-year-old Cro-Magnon girl, and a band of Neanderthals takes her in against their own instincts. Ayla grows up too tall, too pale, too curious, and too willing to do things women of the Clan do not do. The prehistory is exhaustively researched and the politics are pure family drama.

Rejection

Tony Tulathimutte

258 pages

2024
Linked stories about people ruined by wanting to be wanted: the male feminist whose decency is a strategy, the woman optimising herself into nothing, the incel, the striver. Each one escalates past where a polite book would stop, and the final piece turns the whole structure on the reader.

Swan Song

Robert R. McCammon

956 pages

1987
Nuclear war, then the long walk through the ashes. A girl with an inexplicable gift, a wrestler turned protector, a drifter, and a thing wearing many faces that moves among the survivors encouraging the worst in them. A doorstopper apocalypse from the same era as The Stand and unembarrassed about it.

All Fours

Miranda July

326 pages

2024
A semi-famous artist sets out to drive from Los Angeles to New York, gets thirty minutes from home, checks into a motel in Monrovia and stays for three weeks, redecorating the room at enormous expense. What follows is perimenopause as a genuine crisis of self, handled with July's total absence of shame.

A Home at the End of the World

Michael Cunningham

342 pages

1990
Bobby and Jonathan meet as boys in Cleveland, one grieving, one closeted, and stay tangled for decades. Add Clare, older and pregnant, and the three of them try to construct a family with no template for it, in a house upstate, in the years when that was a race against the epidemic.

The Leftovers

Tom Perrotta

355 pages

2011
Two percent of the world's population vanishes at once, and Perrotta ignores the mystery entirely to write about the suburb left behind. A mayor trying to organise a memorial, a wife who joins a cult of silent smokers, a daughter drifting. The rapture as a grief novel about people who were not chosen.

Little Bee

Chris Cleave

304 pages

2008
A Nigerian girl in a British immigration detention centre is released with nowhere to go, and turns up on the doorstep of a magazine editor in Surrey. They met once before, on a beach, on the worst day of both their lives. The book withholds that scene for a long time, and is right to.

The Interestings

Meg Wolitzer

468 pages

2013
Six teenagers meet at an arts summer camp in 1974 and name themselves the Interestings. Wolitzer follows them for forty years as talent turns out to be distributed unevenly and unfairly, and friendship has to survive one of them becoming genuinely famous while the others merely have lives.

Superstud: Or How I Became a 24-Year-Old Virgin

Paul Feig

293 pages

2005
Before Freaks and Geeks and Bridesmaids, Feig catalogues his own humiliations in exhaustive detail: the failed seductions, the medical mishaps, the school dances. Comic memoir that works because he refuses to make himself retroactively cool.

A Visit from the Goon Squad

Jennifer Egan

273 pages

2010
Thirteen chapters moving around a record executive and his former assistant across decades, jumping narrator and form each time, including one told entirely in PowerPoint slides. The goon of the title is time, and the book is about what it does to people who thought they were going to be in music forever.

Bright Shiny Morning

James Frey

501 pages

2008
Los Angeles as a mosaic: a runaway couple, a Mexican maid in Pasadena, an ageing film star, a man living under the freeway, spliced with lists and civic trivia and invented statistics. Frey's first outright novel after the memoir scandal, and deliberately enormous.

The Pisces

Melissa Broder

270 pages

2018
Lucy, dissertation stalled and relationship over, is housesitting in Venice Beach and attending a group therapy for love addicts when she meets a merman on the rocks at night. The sex is transcendent and the rest is a clear-eyed account of using another body to avoid your own mind.

The Invention of Wings

Sue Monk Kidd

373 pages

2014
Charleston, 1803. Sarah Grimke is given a ten-year-old enslaved girl, Handful, as an eleventh-birthday present, and tries to refuse. Thirty-five years of both their lives follow, in alternating voices, toward Sarah's real career as an abolitionist and Handful's own refusals.

From the Mixed-Up Files of Mrs. Basil E. Frankweiler

E.L. Konigsburg

178 pages

1967
Claudia Kincaid, feeling unappreciated, runs away from home to the Metropolitan Museum of Art with her younger brother and his savings, bathing in the fountain and sleeping in a sixteenth-century bed. Then they find a statue that might be a Michelangelo, and go looking for the woman who sold it.

The Twelve

Justin Cronin

568 pages

2012
Book two of The Passage. Five years after the viral collapse, the survivors' settlements are organised, fortified and rotten in places, and the twelve original carriers are still out there with the hive minds they command. Cronin keeps the literary register and widens the scope.

Ice Planet Barbarians

Ruby Dixon

188 pages

2015
Georgie and a cargo hold of abducted human women are dumped on a frozen planet by aliens who no longer want them. Survival requires a symbiont that rewires the body for the cold, and the symbiont has opinions about who belongs to whom. The book that launched a genre's current boom, at 188 unembarrassed pages.

Cat Person and Other Stories

Kristen Roupenian

236 pages

2019
The collection built around the short story that went viral in 2017, about a twenty-year-old, an older man, a bad date and a worse decision. The other pieces are stranger and meaner, running toward outright horror, all of them about the gap between what people want and what they will admit to.

In the Dream House

Carmen Maria Machado

247 pages

2019
Machado's account of an abusive relationship with another woman, told in more than a hundred short chapters each borrowing a narrative form: haunted house, choose-your-own-adventure, folk tale, noir. The structure is the argument, since queer abuse has had no accepted shape to be told in.

Exhalation

Ted Chiang

368 pages

2019
Nine stories, each taking one premise absolutely seriously: a device that proves free will is an illusion, an Arabian gate that leads twenty years into the past, digital pets raised like children, a mechanical being performing an autopsy on its own brain. Chiang's rigour is the pleasure.

Trick Mirror: Reflections on Self-Delusion

Jia Tolentino

303 pages

2019
Nine essays on the internet as an engine for performing the self, plus scamming as the defining ethos of the era, reality television, difficulty in weddings, and the athleisure-optimised body. Tolentino includes herself in every indictment, which is what makes them land.

The Dirt: Confessions of the World's Most Notorious Rock Band

Tommy Lee, Nikki Sixx, Vince Neil

431 pages

2001
Motley Crue in their own alternating voices, and nobody involved has any interest in coming off well. Overdoses, jail, a fatal car crash, heroin, the works, recounted in a deadpan oral-history format that makes the whole thing funnier and considerably worse.

The Hunger Games

Suzanne Collins

374 pages

2008
Katniss volunteers to take her sister's place in a televised fight to the death between children, staged annually by the Capitol as a reminder of a failed rebellion. The genre-defining one, and sharper on spectacle and complicity than its imitators managed.

Gone Girl

Gillian Flynn

463 pages

2012
Amy Dunne disappears on her fifth wedding anniversary and her husband Nick behaves exactly as guilty as everyone needs him to. Her diary tells one story, his narration another, and the midpoint reveal reset what a domestic thriller was allowed to do.

Microserfs

Douglas Coupland

371 pages

1995
Six Microsoft coders in 1993 escape the Redmond campus to build a Lego-like product in a Palo Alto house, narrated in journal entries and fragments of machine noise. Prescient about startup culture, and mostly a book about people trying to get a life while shipping.

How to Be a Person in the World

Heather Havrilesky

258 pages

2016
Selected Ask Polly columns. People write in about jealousy, career panic, bad boyfriends, wanting to be special, and Havrilesky answers at length and at volume, refusing to be soothing while remaining on their side.

Haunted

Chuck Palahniuk

411 pages

2005
Seventeen people answer an ad for a writers' retreat, get locked in a derelict theatre, and set about sabotaging the food and each other so their eventual survival story will sell better. Framed around the stories each tells, including the notorious one about the swimming pool.

Tampa

Alissa Nutting

268 pages

2013
Celeste Price is a beautiful twenty-six-year-old middle school teacher who took the job in order to have access to fourteen-year-old boys, and narrates her predation in gleaming, cold, entirely unrepentant prose. A satire of how attractiveness launders monstrousness.

A Prayer for Owen Meany

John Irving

637 pages

1989
Owen is tiny, has a wrecked voice rendered in capital letters, believes he is God's instrument, and hits a foul ball that kills his best friend's mother. Fifty years of New Hampshire, faith and Vietnam build toward the exact use of everything the book has planted.

The Vampire Lestat

Anne Rice

512 pages

1985
Lestat rises in 1984, becomes a rock star, and writes his own rebuttal to Interview with the Vampire, going back through pre-revolutionary France, his making, and the older things beneath Europe. The book that turned the series into a mythology.

Lamb: The Gospel According to Biff, Christ's Childhood Pal

Christopher Moore

444 pages

2002
Biff is resurrected in a modern hotel room and made to write the missing years of his best friend Joshua's life: the trek east, the yoga, the martial arts, the increasingly nervous run-up to a ministry Biff can see coming. Irreverent, well-researched, and sincerely fond of its subject.

A Clockwork Orange

Anthony Burgess

228 pages

1962
Alex narrates his ultra-violence in Nadsat, a Russian-inflected teen slang the reader has to learn by immersion, then the state cures him of the capacity to choose it. Burgess's question is whether enforced goodness is worth anything at all.

Room

Emma Donoghue

10h 46m audio

2010
Jack is five and Room is the whole world: eleven feet square, with Ma, and Old Nick who comes at night. Narrated entirely in Jack's voice, which makes the first half bearable and the second half, once there is an outside, much harder.

Under the Banner of Heaven

Jon Krakauer

400 pages

2003
The 1984 murder of a woman and her infant by two fundamentalist Mormon brothers acting on revelation, braided with a history of the LDS church from Joseph Smith through the Mountain Meadows massacre. Krakauer's argument is about what faith licenses.

Gone at Midnight: The Mysterious Death of Elisa Lam

Jake Anderson

368 pages

2020
The Canadian student found in a water tank on the roof of LA's Cecil Hotel, and the elevator footage that made her a decade of internet theorising. Anderson works the case against his own obsession with it, and lands on mental illness and institutional failure rather than the occult.

Disappearing Earth

Julia Phillips

256 pages

2019
Two sisters vanish from a beach in Kamchatka, and the following chapters move month by month through the women of the peninsula, most of them strangers to the case, until the connections close. As much about indigenous Even communities and Russian indifference as about the crime.

The Giver

Lois Lowry

179 pages

1993
Jonas turns twelve and is assigned to be Receiver of Memory, the one person permitted to know what his community traded away for its total calm: colour, weather, music, grief, choice. The ending is still argued about.

The Neverending Story

Michael Ende

448 pages

1979
Bastian steals a book, hides in his school attic, and reads about Fantastica being eaten by the Nothing, until the book starts addressing him directly and he goes in. The second half, which the film abandoned, is about what unlimited wishing costs.

The Idiot

Elif Batuman

423 pages

2017
Selin's first year at Harvard in 1995: linguistics, email as a brand new medium, and a slow oblique correspondence with an older Hungarian mathematics student that goes nowhere and consumes everything. Very funny about being clever and completely unequipped.

The Gum Thief

Douglas Coupland

275 pages

2007
Roger, divorced and fifty-ish, works at a Staples and writes journal entries in the voice of Bethany, the goth twenty-something on the next till. She finds the notebook. What follows is a correspondence, plus the terrible novel Roger is writing inside it.

All Families are Psychotic

Douglas Coupland

288 pages

2001
The Drummond family converges on Florida to watch their daughter, a one-armed astronaut, launch. Within days there is a kidnapping, a black-market fetus, several guns and multiple HIV diagnoses, delivered at farce speed and with real tenderness underneath.

The City of Mirrors

Justin Cronin

624 pages

2016
The Passage trilogy closes. The virals have been quiet for twenty years and the settlements have started to believe it, which is when Zero, the first of them, finally gets to explain himself, at length, as a love story. Then the last siege.

Old Futures: Speculative Fiction and Queer Possibility

Alexis Lothian

352 pages

2018
An academic study of the futures that got discarded: feminist utopias, Black speculative writing, queer fan fiction, early twentieth-century eugenic fantasies. Lothian reads what a culture imagines as evidence of what it intends.

Sex, Drugs, and Cocoa Puffs: A Low Culture Manifesto

Chuck Klosterman

253 pages

2003
Essays taking cereal, Saved by the Bell, Pamela Anderson, tribute bands and the Sims as seriously as anyone has, on the premise that pop culture is where people actually learn how to think about love and morality. Very much of its decade, and still funny.

Dancing with the Octopus: A Memoir of a Crime

Debora Harding

384 pages

2020
At fourteen Harding was abducted at knifepoint from a church car park, raped, and left tied in a rail yard in a blizzard. Decades later she goes looking for the man who did it, and finds the harder subject is her mother, whose cruelty the family never named.

A Confederacy of Dunces

John Kennedy Toole

394 pages

1980
Ignatius J. Reilly, thirty, medievalist, valve-afflicted, living with his mother in New Orleans, is forced to seek employment and inflicts himself on the city instead. Published eleven years after Toole's suicide and won the Pulitzer.

Bound to the Battle God

Ruby Dixon

656 pages

2019
Aspect and Anchor book one. Piety is a temple acolyte who botches a ritual and finds herself soul-bound to Kassam, the god of war, which means sharing a body, a mind and a very bad temper while they try to undo it. Fantasy romance with Dixon's usual banter and considerable length.

All's Well

Mona Awad

368 pages

2021
Miranda, a former actress with chronic pain nobody believes in, teaches theatre and is losing a battle to stage All's Well That Ends Well instead of Macbeth. Then three strange men in a bar transfer her suffering to everyone doubting it. Awad on pain, gaslighting and the pleasure of revenge.

Seek You: A Journey Through American Loneliness

Kristen Radtke

352 pages

2021
An illustrated essay on loneliness as an American condition, moving through Harry Harlow's monkey experiments, laugh tracks, gun ownership, the loneliness of marriage and Radtke's own family. The art carries the argument as much as the text.

Last Call: A True Story of Love, Lust, and Murder in Queer New York

Elon Green

255 pages

2021
A serial killer picked up middle-aged men from Manhattan piano bars in the early nineties and left them dismembered along highways, and almost nobody covered it. Green gives each victim a full life first, which is the point of the book.

Detransition, Baby

Torrey Peters

340 pages

2021
Reese wants a baby. Her ex Amy has detransitioned back to Ames, got his cis boss Katrina pregnant, and proposes the three of them raise it together. Sharp, unsentimental, and written entirely for trans women rather than about them.

The School for Good Mothers

Jessamine Chan

336 pages

2022
Frida leaves her toddler alone for two hours on one bad day and loses her to a state programme that retrains mothers using android dolls and metrics. The satire is thin enough over current family policing to be genuinely upsetting.

One Thousand White Women: The Journals of May Dodd

Jim Fergus

304 pages

1998
Built on a real 1854 proposal, that the Cheyenne requested a thousand white women as brides to assimilate their peoples, and the counterfactual that the US agreed and recruited from asylums and prisons. May Dodd's journals run from the trains west to Sand Creek.

The Red Tent

Anita Diamant

324 pages

1997
Dinah gets one violent verse in Genesis and no voice; Diamant gives her a life, told through the women's tent where the wives of Jacob menstruate, birth and gossip. The Shechem episode arrives as something entirely other than what scripture says it was.

Such a Fun Age

Kiley Reid

305 pages

2019
Emira, twenty-five and Black, is accused of kidnapping the white toddler she babysits while at a supermarket at midnight, and a bystander films it. Her employer Alix decides to make it right, which is where the trouble actually starts. Excruciating on white good intentions.

The Other Boleyn Girl

Philippa Gregory

664 pages

2001
Mary Boleyn is Henry VIII's mistress first, and then watches her family reload and aim her sister at him. Narrated by the sister who survived, which turns the Tudor court into a family business trading daughters at the going rate.

Olga Dies Dreaming

Xochitl Gonzalez

373 pages

2022
Olga plans weddings for Manhattan billionaires and her brother is a closeted congressman, and their mother, who left to join the Puerto Rican independence movement, writes them letters full of judgement. Then Hurricane Maria hits. Family novel with the politics fully on the page.

The Secret History

Donna Tartt

559 pages

1992
Richard talks his way into an elite Greek class at a Vermont college and into the group around it, who have already done something unforgivable and will do worse to protect it. The murder is on page one; the dread is entirely about consequence. The gold standard of the form.

We Keep the Dead Close

Becky Cooper

499 pages

2020
A Harvard archaeology student was killed in her apartment in 1969 and the rumour said a professor did it. Cooper spends ten years on the story, and the book becomes about the institution's protection of its own and her own difficulty letting go.

My Dark Vanessa

Kate Elizabeth Russell

372 pages

2020
At fifteen Vanessa was groomed by her boarding school English teacher; at thirty-two, with other women coming forward, she is still defending it as the great love of her life. The narration never breaks from her insistence, which is exactly the horror.

The End of Mr. Y

Scarlett Thomas

402 pages

2006
A PhD student finds a cursed nineteenth-century novel that nobody who reads it survives, follows its recipe, and ends up in the Troposphere, a space made of consciousness where you can travel through other minds. Thought experiments and theology at thriller pace.

I'm Thinking of Ending Things

Iain Reid

183 pages

2016
A woman drives with her new boyfriend to his parents' remote farm, planning to break up with him, and the visit goes wrong in ways that keep sliding sideways. Short, claustrophobic, and the ending recontextualises every page.

Station Eleven

Emily St. John Mandel

333 pages

2014
A flu kills most of the world during a production of King Lear, and twenty years later a travelling company performs Shakespeare around the Great Lakes because survival is insufficient. Moves back and forth around a comic book and the people it touched.

The Passage

Justin Cronin

784 pages

2010
A military virus trial produces twelve immortal carriers, they get out, and America falls in about a hundred pages. Then a jump of a century to a walled colony in California running out of light, and a girl who does not age. Vampire apocalypse written with total literary seriousness.

In the Quick

Kate Hope Day

272 pages

2021
June is a prodigy raised by an uncle who designed the fuel cells for a lost spacecraft, and she works her way into the space programme convinced the missing crew is alive. Reworks Jane Eyre as an engineering story about obsession.

Uncanny Valley

Anna Wiener

288 pages

2020
Wiener leaves New York publishing for startup customer support in San Francisco in the mid-2010s, and reports back on the analytics company, the equity, the vests, the men, and the growing sense that the whole apparatus was aimed at nothing worth building.

Raised in Captivity: Fictional Nonfiction

Chuck Klosterman

320 pages

2019
Very short stories built as thought experiments: a puma in an aeroplane lavatory, a man who can hear a sound nobody else can, a cure for cruelty. Klosterman's essayistic instinct applied to fiction, ending most pieces one beat before the resolution.

The Cabin at the End of the World

Paul Tremblay

272 pages

2018
Two dads and their daughter are at a lake cabin when four strangers arrive and explain, apologetically, that the family must choose one of themselves to kill or the world ends. Tremblay never confirms whether they are right, and the book is unbearable for it.

The Indifferent Stars Above: The Harrowing Saga of a Donner Party Bride

Daniel James Brown

337 pages

2009
The Donner Party through Sarah Graves, married weeks before departure, and Brown supplies the physiology to go with it: what starvation does in what order, how long a body lasts in snow. Clear-eyed rather than lurid, which makes it worse.

Dark Matter

Blake Crouch

342 pages

2016
A physics professor is abducted, drugged, and wakes in a version of his life where he made the other choice, celebrated and childless. Getting home means navigating a box that opens onto every branch of every decision. Ruthlessly paced.

Recursion

Blake Crouch

329 pages

2019
People are developing memories of lives they never lived, and a NYPD detective and a neuroscientist working on memory preservation converge on why. Escalates from a mystery into something structurally reckless, and Crouch commits to the consequences.

Homebound

Lydia Hope

539 pages

2020
Earth is in decline and anyone who could leave has. Gemma serves meals at a prison and gets reassigned to the alien ward, where Simon, a Rix left starving in isolation, despises humans and wants only to get off the planet before the scientist hunting his genes catches up. She nurses him back anyway.

The Rabbit Hutch

Tess Gunty

338 pages

2022
A decaying apartment block in a dying Indiana factory town, and Blandine, eighteen, out of foster care, reading mystics and planning something. Structurally restless, mixing obituary comment threads and stage directions, and it earned the National Book Award.

The Last Hour of Gann

R. Lee Smith

706 pages

2013
Amber boards Earth's first colony ship and wakes in its wreck on an unmapped world with no rescue coming. The survivors are found by Meoraq, a holy warrior of a reptilian people, sworn to judgement and on the last pilgrimage of his free life. Brutal and unsparing about what survival costs.

Piranesi

Susanna Clarke

245 pages

2020
A man lives in an infinite house of statues and tides, keeps meticulous journals, and knows there are fifteen people in the world, thirteen of them skeletons. He is content. The book is the slow arrival of the knowledge that will end that.

You

Caroline Kepnes

432 pages

2014
Joe works in a bookshop, meets Beck, and narrates his stalking of her directly to her in second person, reasonable and charming and appalling throughout. The device is the whole trick and it works because he keeps almost convincing you.

Youth in Revolt

C.D. Payne

512 pages

1993
The journals of Nick Twisp, fourteen, pretentious, relentlessly horny and increasingly criminal in pursuit of Sheeni Saunders. Escalates from teenage grievance to arson and multi-state flight without ever losing the diary voice.

Fantasticland

Mike Bockoven

272 pages

2016
A hurricane cuts off a Florida theme park with its staff inside, and five weeks later they are extracted having split into warring tribes by costume department. Told as an oral history of interviews after the fact, which keeps the reader assembling it.

Tell Me I'm Worthless

Alison Rumfitt

264 pages

2021
Three years ago Alice and Ila went into a derelict house and came out with incompatible accounts of what happened. The house is fascism, literally and not metaphorically, and it wants them back. Furious, formally aggressive, and explicit about transphobia as a haunting.

No books match that combination.
